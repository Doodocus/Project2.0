Focus

Three questions behind the work

How language models fail, how those failures can be measured, and how safer systems can be built around them.

  • LLM security

    How tool-using agents can be steered by untrusted content, and which simple defenses help in a controlled setting.

Selected research

Four pieces of fictional research

Titles, statuses, and findings are mock data. None of these are peer-reviewed publications, and there are no papers to download.

  1. Research paper 2026 · Submitted to a fictional student research workshop

    “Evidence Before Fluency: Measuring Unsupported Claims in Retrieval-Augmented Language Models”

    Contribution
    Co-designed a test set where retrieved documents are incomplete, conflicting, or irrelevant, then compared answer correctness with whether each claim was actually supported by the sources.
    Takeaway
    A fluent answer can still overstate what its sources establish; claim-level evidence checks make that gap easier to see.

  2. Preprint 2025 · Fictional

    “Prompt Injection Across the Tool Boundary: A Small-Scale Study of Agent Defenses”

    Contribution
    Built a controlled set of indirect prompt-injection scenarios for tool-using agents and compared simple isolation and tool-permission strategies.
    Takeaway
    Narrow tool permissions and explicit separation of untrusted content reduced successful attacks in the study’s toy environment.

  3. Poster 2025 · Fictional MIT Undergraduate Research Symposium

    “When ‘I Don’t Know’ Helps: Calibrating Abstention in Student-Facing Assistants”

    Contribution
    Evaluated when an educational assistant should answer, cite a source, or abstain, using a small synthetic question set and a human review rubric.
    Takeaway
    Abstention quality depends on giving users a useful next step, not simply refusing uncertain questions.

  4. Technical report 2024 · Fictional

    “A Practical Taxonomy of Hallucination Reports for Product Teams”

    Contribution
    Organized user-reported failures into categories such as unsupported detail, source mismatch, stale information, and false certainty.
    Takeaway
    A consistent taxonomy helps teams prioritize fixes and compare failures across product releases.

About

From “it sounds right” to measurably reliable

Alex is pursuing a B.S. in Computer Science at MIT, expected May 2027, and is based in Cambridge, Massachusetts. He became interested in language-model reliability after seeing how confident, plausible answers can still be wrong.

Since then he has focused on testing model behavior under adversarial prompts, tracing claims to source material, and communicating uncertainty to users. Outside the lab, he enjoys turning research prototypes into useful products and has co-founded a small student venture exploring reliability tools for AI developers.

His long-term goal is to help research labs and product teams move from “the model seems to work” to measurable, monitorable reliability.
